\section{Background}
\acp{dtn} are networks in which a continuous path between source and destination cannot be assumed. Nodes store messages, carry them while moving, and forward them opportunistically when contacts occur. This store-carry-forward model is useful in disaster-response scenarios because damaged infrastructure, sparse deployment, intermittent wireless links, and node mobility make conventional Internet-style routing impractical \citep{cerf2007dtn}. In such environments, communication support must continue even when connectivity is fragmented.

The project studies routing for a disaster-response \ac{dtn} consisting of civilians, responders, shelters, and drones. Each role has different mobility and operational meaning. Civilians move slowly and may carry local reports. Responders move faster and are expected to participate in rescue operations. Shelters are static destinations or coordination points. Drones are fast mobile relays that can bridge separated areas. A routing protocol that treats all of these nodes identically ignores information that is already available in the scenario.

The protocol proposed in this thesis is called Spatio-Semantic routing. The term spatial refers to location, zone visitation, and physical proximity to the destination. The term semantic refers to the operational role of the node and the priority of the message. The protocol combines both dimensions with encounter history and transitive delivery prediction. Its aim is not simply to maximize all message delivery, but to preserve the delivery of critical messages when traffic load increases and buffers become congested.

\section{Need for Delay Tolerant Routing}
Traditional \ac{manet} routing protocols generally depend on the existence or discovery of a contemporaneous path. This assumption is weak in sparse, mobile, and damaged-network conditions. In contrast, \ac{dtn} routing accepts that contacts may be rare and that the best relay at the present time may only be useful because of where it is likely to move later. Routing therefore becomes a decision problem: when two nodes meet, each protocol must decide which messages should be copied, how many copies should be transferred, and whether the receiver is more useful than the current carrier.

Three broad routing behaviors are relevant to this project. Epidemic routing floods messages to every encountered node that does not already hold the message \citep{vahdat2000epidemic}. Spray and Wait limits the number of copies and then waits for a direct encounter with the destination \citep{spyropoulos2005spray}. Prediction-based protocols such as \ac{prophet} use encounter history and transitivity to forward to more promising carriers \citep{lindgren2012prophet}. The proposed protocol draws from these ideas but adds spatial zones, semantic node roles, critical-message buffer reservation, and hybrid copy allocation.

\section{Motivation}
The practical motivation is emergency communication. In a disaster area, messages are not equally important. A medical alert, shelter request, or responder coordination update should not be treated like routine background information. A routing protocol that achieves high average delivery but loses urgent messages under congestion is unsuitable for this use case.

The technical motivation is that existing baselines expose opposite weaknesses. Epidemic routing can deliver well in light traffic, but its uncontrolled replication creates high overhead and buffer pressure. Spray and Wait controls overhead, but its conservative forwarding can delay or miss urgent messages when the limited copies are held by weak carriers. The proposed protocol attempts to occupy the useful middle ground: it forwards aggressively when a message is critical or when enough copies exist, but it becomes selective as copies become scarce.

\section{Scope of the Project}
The scope of the project includes the design, implementation, and simulation-based evaluation of a spatio-semantic routing protocol for a disaster-response \ac{dtn}. The implementation is written in Python and compares three protocols:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Epidemic routing as the flooding baseline.
	\item Spray and Wait routing as the copy-limited baseline.
	\item Spatio-Semantic routing as the proposed protocol.
\end{itemize}

The simulator models a square disaster area, wireless contact range, finite node buffers, message generation under three traffic levels, critical and non-critical messages, random waypoint mobility, static shelters, and fast drones. The evaluation uses delivery ratio, critical delivery ratio, average delay, average critical delay, overhead ratio, and buffer drops.

The project does not implement a real wireless deployment, cryptographic security, energy consumption, interference modeling, or real map-based mobility. These are outside the current thesis scope. The results should therefore be interpreted as simulation evidence for the protocol design rather than as direct field deployment measurements.

\section{Disaster Communication Scenario Considered}
The scenario considered in this work is a post-disaster area where normal communication infrastructure is either unavailable or unreliable. The network is not assumed to have base stations, routers, or stable Internet access. Instead, communication depends on devices carried by people and mobile agents. A civilian may generate a message requesting help, a responder may carry operational information, a shelter may receive status updates, and a drone may move quickly across otherwise disconnected parts of the area.

This setting is different from a normal wireless network. In a normal network, the routing problem is often to find the shortest path or the least-cost path through an already connected graph. In the disaster setting, the graph itself appears and disappears over time. Two nodes are connected only when they are physically close enough to communicate. A path that exists at one time step may vanish immediately after the nodes move. Because of this, forwarding a message to a node is also a bet on that node's future movement.

The model used in this project is intentionally simple, but the simplicity is useful. It allows the behavior of the routing protocols to be compared without adding too many uncontrolled factors. The simulator does not attempt to reproduce every detail of a real disaster zone. Instead, it captures the main pressure points that matter for the thesis: intermittent contact, limited buffers, heterogeneous mobility, critical traffic, and increasing congestion.

\section{Why Spatial Information Matters}
In a \ac{dtn}, the current position of a node is only one part of its usefulness. A node that is close to the destination now may move away before it can deliver. Another node that is farther away may regularly visit the destination area and may therefore be a better carrier. For this reason, the proposed protocol does not depend only on physical distance. It also stores zone visit history.

The use of zones is a practical compromise. A protocol could try to store full movement traces, but this would increase memory use and make the design more complicated. Dividing the region into zones gives the router a compact summary of movement. If a node repeatedly visits the same zone as the destination, that node becomes more attractive as a relay. In the simulated disaster setting, this is especially useful because shelters are fixed and drones or responders may repeatedly travel around them.

Spatial information also helps avoid one weakness of blind forwarding. Epidemic routing treats a nearby but unhelpful contact almost the same as a contact that is likely to move toward the destination. That can be acceptable when buffers are large and traffic is light, but it becomes wasteful when the network is congested. The proposed protocol tries to spend copies on receivers whose movement pattern suggests future usefulness.

\section{Why Semantic Information Matters}
The semantic part of the protocol is based on the idea that node roles carry meaning. A drone is not only a point in space; it is a fast relay. A shelter is not only a static node; it is a likely sink for emergency coordination. A responder is not only a moving node; it is an operationally important participant. Ignoring these meanings forces the routing protocol to learn everything indirectly through contacts, even when some useful information is already known.

In this project, semantic information is deliberately kept lightweight. The protocol does not require a complex ontology, natural language understanding, or global command system. It only uses the role assigned to each node and the criticality flag attached to each message. This makes the idea easier to implement and easier to reason about. The goal is not to build a fully intelligent emergency network, but to show that even a small amount of semantic awareness can improve routing behavior.

The message priority is also semantic information. A critical message should receive different treatment from a routine message. In a real disaster response, this distinction is natural. A message about immediate danger, injury, shelter overload, or responder coordination should not be dropped simply because less important messages arrived first. The proposed buffer reserve is a direct response to this concern.

\section{Thesis Contribution in Context}
The main contribution of the thesis is the combination of spatial and semantic decision-making inside a copy-based \ac{dtn} router. The protocol does not reject the lessons of existing methods. It borrows the reachability benefit of spraying, the selectivity of utility-based forwarding, and the predictive value of encounter history. Its novelty in this project is how these are combined for a disaster-response scenario with role-aware nodes and priority-aware messages.

The comparison with Epidemic routing and Spray and Wait is important because it makes the trade-off visible. Epidemic routing shows what happens when the network tries to maximize spread without discipline. Spray and Wait shows what happens when the network controls copies but uses a simple forwarding rule. Spatio-Semantic routing is designed to show a more balanced approach: it is willing to spend transmissions, but it tries to spend them on better carriers and more important messages.

The thesis therefore does not claim that one protocol is universally superior for every network. A very small network with almost no congestion may not need this added logic. A network with strict energy constraints may prefer an even more conservative method. The claim is narrower and more defensible: for a congested disaster-response \ac{dtn}, where critical messages matter and node roles are known, a spatio-semantic protocol gives a better emergency communication trade-off than the two baselines used in this project.

\section{Organization of the Thesis}
The remaining chapters develop this argument step by step. Chapter 2 discusses existing \ac{dtn} routing work, identifies the gap addressed by this project, and states the project objectives. Chapter 3 describes the technical specification, including functional requirements, system assumptions, simulation parameters, and performance metrics. Chapter 4 presents the system design and explains the utility function, forwarding phases, and buffer policy used by the proposed protocol.

Chapter 5 explains the methodology and testing procedure. It describes the traffic scenarios, repeated experiment process, visual dashboard, and threats to validity. Chapter 6 describes the implementation files and explains how messages, nodes, routers, and result files are handled in code. Chapter 8 concludes the thesis, summarizes the contribution, and suggests further enhancements.

This structure is intended to keep the thesis readable. The early chapters establish the problem, the middle chapters explain the design and implementation, and the later chapters evaluate the outcome. This is important because the value of the proposed protocol does not come from one isolated formula. It comes from how the scenario, design choices, simulation, and results fit together.
